## Voice summary

Wright Co. talks like Cody explaining the job over a tailgate: short sentences, the customer's own words, and the work laid out in the order it happens. The words are plain and neighborly ("we'll walk it with you", "here's what drives the price"), and the confidence comes from describing the job concretely, never from adjectives about the company. It speaks to people who just bought land and feel in over their heads, so it explains once, kindly, and never makes anyone feel dumb for not knowing what a culvert permit is. It is serious about the work and the money, a little dry and gritty on Facebook, and it would always rather show a finished pad than say "quality".

**The voice in one sentence**

> Wright Co. sounds like the neighbor with the equipment who walks your land with you: plain-spoken, straight about the hard parts, and quietly sure he can get it ready to build.

**Where it comes from.** The owner's intake sentence is the voice: "We come in and clear your land trees and stumps, then we put in a driveway in to future home site. Then excavate for the house foundation. We do everything start to finish." Keep "we come in", the "then... then", "your land" and the zero hype. Fix the grammar only. Never polish the sequence out of it.

**Three rules**

1. **Walk the order.** First, then, then. The product is the sequence, so the copy is a walkthrough.
2. **Show it, don't claim it.** Specifics and (later) real lots carry the confidence. No adjectives about ourselves.
3. **Describe only Wright Co.** No sentence says what another outfit does or fails to do.

**The test for any line:** if it sounds like Cody's intake sentence cleaned up, it's on voice. If it sounds like the current website hero ("Built on Hard Work. Driven by Quality."), it isn't.

**The claims test:** can we show it on a real Louisiana job, or is it on the approved list in file 6? If not, cut it or mark it `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING`.

## Core voice traits

Seven qualities. Every "do" is a draft.

### Plain-spoken

What it means: say it the way the buyer would say it to their brother-in-law. If a word would sound strange said out loud in a feed store, cut it.

- **Do:** "We clear it, cut in the driveway and build your house pad."
- **Don't:** "We deliver comprehensive site preparation solutions for residential projects."

### Sequenced

What it means: the copy walks the order. Numbered steps are the house style.

- **Do:** "First we clear the trees and stumps. Then we cut in the driveway so trucks can get in. Then we build the pad."
- **Don't:** a grid of six unrelated service tiles with one adjective each.

### Straight

What it means: name the hard parts early: rain, fill distance, soft ground, and what's on the owner (911 address, culvert permit, survey). Answer "how much?" with what drives the price before any number.

- **Do:** "Price comes down to five things: acreage, how thick the trees are, stumps, how far the fill hauls, and how high the pad needs to be."
- **Don't:** "Affordable rates! Call for pricing!"

### Quietly confident

What it means: confidence is shown by being specific. The finished lot is the brag. No superlatives, no badges.

- **Do:** "Here's a lot outside Independence: woods in March, pad ready for the builder in April." (A real job only. None exists yet.)
- **Don't:** "Hammond's premier, top-rated land clearing experts."

### Local, without the costume

What it means: name the towns, the parishes and the ground: Hammond, Ponchatoula, Independence, Amite, Loranger, Albany; clay, low corners that hold water, pine and sweetgum. Specificity is the local proof. Tenure is not.

- **Do:** "Serving Hammond, Tangipahoa Parish & the Northshore. We know a low corner when we see one."
- **Don't:** "Proudly serving Hammond for years!" or Cajun caricature: gators, "cher", "laissez les bons temps". Hammond is the Florida Parishes, not Acadiana.

### Respectful

What it means: most customers have never done this. Explain once, kindly, and move on. Never correct them; use the right word back.

- **Do:** "Good question. A lot of folks don't know the culvert comes first. Here's how it works in Tangipahoa."
- **Don't:** "Obviously you'll need your culvert permit before we can start."

### A little grit

What it means: the emblem is distressed varsity lettering on Louisiana dirt, so the voice can have a little mud on its boots: short fragments, physical verbs, dry humor in social.

- **Do:** "Stumps out. Driveway in. Pad up."
- **Don't:** swagger ("We crush land!"), outlaw energy, or anything that sounds like it would leave ruts in your field.

## Writing rules

- Use active voice, with "we" as the subject. Passive only when someone else really does it: "The culvert is sized by the parish."
- Address the customer directly: "you", "your land", "your lot".
- Keep sentences short: 8 to 15 words on the web, under 10 in social and SMS. One idea per sentence; one to three sentences per paragraph. Turn long explanations into numbered steps.
- **Contractions: always.** Uncontracted only for emphasis: "We do not set your elevation. Your surveyor does."
- **Person:** "we" for the company. "I" only when Cody is literally texting, calling or signing. "You" for the reader. The owner is "Cody Wright" on first mention in formal copy and "Cody" after and in social.
- **Slang:** "folks" yes. "Y'all" sparingly, in social and spoken copy only.
- **Humor:** dry, short, about the ground or the work ("That stump had opinions."). Never in estimates, scopes, weather messages, money, water, or aimed at the customer.
- **Profanity:** never. The sources do not address it; this is the default.
- **Emoji:** none on the website, in graphics, headings or buttons. Zero to two in a Facebook caption at most. None in SMS strings.
- **Exclamation marks:** one per page or post at most, preferably none. Never in a heading or a button. Never in a text from Cody unless he's replying to a customer's good news.
- **Dashes:** em dashes sparingly in web copy, one per section at most; none in SMS or estimates. Prefer a period.
- **Ellipses:** no.
- **Arrows and middots** are house style for sequences and descriptors: clear → driveway → pad; Clear · Driveway · House Pad.
- **Trade words:** use them, then explain once: "a culvert (the pipe under your driveway so the ditch keeps draining)". "Select fill", "60/40", "BFE" and "lift" are explained at first use, or not used.
- No capitals for emphasis. No asterisk bullet lists.
- No rhetorical question with the brand as the answer. A question is fine when it's the customer's real question: "How high should my pad be?"
- Every factual claim has a source someone could check. If it doesn't, leave a bracketed note for the editor and do not fill the gap.

## Brand-specific language

- **The Homeplace Package.** Always with its descriptor: *"Clear · Driveway · House Pad. One crew, one scope, build-ready."* Never shown alone. Whether the foundation dig, culvert and fill are inside it is `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING`; until then: "Ask what's included for your lot."
- **The Free Lot Walk.** "Book a Free Lot Walk" on the button. "We'll do a lot walk" in running prose is fine. Keep "Free" only while it truly is. Whether Cody walks every lot in person, how far out, and whether he leaves a one-page plan are `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING`.
- **The Flag Walk.** A moment inside the Lot Walk, not a separate offer: "We'll flag your driveway and pad line." `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING` with the Lot Walk.
- **The Dirt Order.** Always with its subtitle: *"Building on Raw Land in Tangipahoa, Step by Step."* Series label: "Dirt Order #3: How high should my pad be?"
- **The sequence.** 01 Clear · 02 Driveway · 03 Pad · 04 Foundation Dig. Same words, same order, every time.
- **The customer.** "You", "folks", "landowners". Builders are "builders". Never "clients" on the homeowner side, never "users".
- **The crew.** "We", "our crew". Never "our team of professionals". No crew size.
- **"The Wright way".** Held. If the owner confirms it is his line, **one** use in the whole system (an apparel print or a sign-off: "Ready to build. The Wright way."), never an H1 or an offer name. `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING`.

## Headline style

Plain statements in sentence case that name the buyer's moment, the step, or the customer's real question. Graduate sets them in capitals, so they must be short: six to eight words, two lines at most. No superlative, no pun, no exclamation mark.

Examples (all drafts):

- "You bought the land. We'll get it ready to build."
- "From the first tree to the house pad."
- "Just closed on land? Here's what happens first."
- "One crew. Your land. In order."
- "Land clearing in Hammond, with the stumps out"
- "House pads, built to your survey"
- "What moves the price"
- "Stumps out. Driveway in. Pad up."

## CTA style

Two buttons, exactly these words, everywhere. The phone is a button, not a bare link, and the two are equal width.

Preferred:

- **"Book a Free Lot Walk"** (primary, solid Cattail Rust)
- **"Call or Text 360-957-5865"** (secondary, outlined, linked `tel:+13609575865`; in a narrow header it may read "Call or Text" with the number in its link)
- A text link that names its destination: "Read The Dirt Order", "Ask about the Homeplace Package", "See what moves the price"
- In a sentence: "Call or text Cody at 360-957-5865, or book a free lot walk."

Avoid:

- "Get Started", "Learn More", "Contact Us", "Submit", "Click Here"
- "Get a Quote", "Request Pricing", "See Rates", "View Pricing"
- "Call Now!", "Book Now", "Limited Time", "Only 2 Spots Left", any countdown
- "Talk to an Expert", "Speak to Our Team"
- "Get Your Lot Build-Ready" or any other invented label
- Any "Wright" pun on a button

### Phone script, inbound (draft)

1. "Wright Co., this is Cody."
2. "Tell me about the land. Where's it at, and what are you hoping to put on it?"
3. Qualify with the sequence: wooded or open; roughly how many acres; a 911 address yet; a pad height from the builder or surveyor; when the builder wants to start.
4. "Here's how it usually goes. We clear it first, trees and stumps. Then we cut in the driveway so trucks can get in. Then we build the pad up to the height on your survey. One crew the whole way."
5. On price: "I can't give you a fair number over the phone. It comes down to acreage, how thick the trees are, the stumps, how far we haul fill and how high the pad goes. Let me come walk it and I'll put it in writing."
6. Close on the lot walk: "I can come out [day]. We'll walk it together." Adding "and I'll flag where the driveway and pad would go. No charge." is `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING`.

Tricky questions:

- *"How long have you been doing this?"* "I've been clearing and excavating since 2022." Where he worked before is `CONFIRM BEFORE PUBLISHING`. Never years in Louisiana or Hammond.
- *"Are you licensed and insured?"* Cody answers personally with whatever is true at the time. A crew member says: "Cody handles that. Let me have him call you." Never guess.
- *"Do you have reviews?"* "We're new to the area, so not many yet. Judge us on the work."
- *"Can you set my pad height?"* "That's your surveyor and the parish floodplain office. We build to what they give you."

## Never

1. A credential nobody can check: licensed, insured, bonded, "LLC", a license number.
2. A history nobody can check: years in Hammond or Louisiana, "established", "decades", a founding story.
3. A rating, a review count, or anyone else's words as ours. No persona "quote" from a research document published as a testimonial. Nothing from the owner's Washington business.
4. A grade of ourselves: "premier", "#1", "best", "trusted", "top-rated".
5. A grade of anyone else, or a competitor's phrasing.
6. A number nobody approved: prices, ranges, per-acre rates, durations, lot counts.
7. A promise about what the weather, the parish or the surveyor controls: dates, dry pads, elevations, permits.
8. A promise the owner hasn't made: firm price, callback times, culvert install, in-house hauling, what's in the package.
9. A street address, or "based in".
10. Pressure: countdowns, "limited", fake deadlines, more than one nudge on a cold lead.
11. Costume: Cajun dialect, gators, swagger.
12. More than one "Wright" pun in the whole system, and none until the owner confirms it.
